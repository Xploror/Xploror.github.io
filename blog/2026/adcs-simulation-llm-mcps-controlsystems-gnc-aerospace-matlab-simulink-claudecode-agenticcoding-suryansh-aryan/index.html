One of the biggest assets an #ADCS engineer can have is an all-purpose simulation tool that can support distinct scenarios and compare various control law performances while still having a flexibility to tune the #simulation setup, dynamics and controller gains. This has motivated me to professionally design a MATLAB/Simulink application specifically comparing three attitude control schemes under four distinct scenarios while a chaser rendezvous with the ISS.

The attitude is modelled with quaternion kinematics and rigid-body dynamics with reaction-wheel actuation and it’s gyroscopic coupling. Direction-cosine transformations move vectors between inertial, body and reference frames, and the error quaternion is split into pointing error and twist about the docking axis. The selected controllers for the comparison are, a robust sliding-mode law, an adaptive sliding-mode law with inertia estimator (with parameter projection and anti-windup), and a quaternion PD baseline controller. It has two interchangeable simulation engines: a generated Simulink model (default), and a built-in fixed-step RK4 MATLAB reference engine, both agreeing to about 1e-11 degrees on the deterministic cases. The Simulink model is auto-generated by scripts, therefore capable of build reproducibility. A test suite of 9 automated tests passes, and a smoke test runs every scenario across all three controllers: 20 of 21 runs pass with identical verdicts on both engines.

A year ago for my research projects, I had designed these necessary MATLAB functions and Simulink block diagrams for both adaptive SMC and PD controllers for a typical co-planer along-track LEO rendezvous with exponential and sinusoidal docking axis approach profile. However, while designing the MATLAB app, formalizing my codebase to be distribution-ready and easily accessible to already existing #LLM agents turned out to be strenuous. I trust missing data more than misleading data in orbital systems

I recently built a re-entry risk screening feature in DRAKON, and the hardest part wasn’t the orbital mechanics, it was deciding what not to trust.

Most approaches rely on the BSTAR drag term from TLEs to estimate orbital decay. On paper, it looks like a clean signal. In reality, BSTAR is a least-squares fitting coefficient computed by the SGP4 orbit determination process from radar tracking arcs. it absorbs not just atmospheric drag, but also maneuver residuals and tracking noise.

This creates a failure mode that’s easy to miss: active satellites can appear as high-risk re-entry objects simply because they recently performed a maneuver. At one point, operational Starlink satellites were showing up as “~25 day to re-entry” - Numerically coherent. Physically meaningless.

The solution wasn’t a better formula. It was a change in assumptions.

Instead of trying to extract signal from noisy physics, I filtered the problem at the system level:

Only debris and rocket bodies are screened All likely active satellites are treated as non-decaying by default Aggressive sanity gates remove anomalous BSTAR-driven estimates

This is a deliberate tradeoff: precision over recall. It’s better to miss a few edge cases than to flood the system with confident-looking false positives.

There were similar tradeoffs on the computation side. Re-entry risk is derived from static TLE parameters, not live propagation - so the entire pipeline runs synchronously over ~15K objects in sub-millisecond time, avoiding unnecessary recomputation and UI lag.

The broader lesson here is something I keep running into with orbital data:

The challenge is rarely just modeling the physics. It’s understanding where the data stops being trustworthy and designing the system around that boundary.

In many cases, the most important decision isn’t how to compute something. It’s whether you should compute it at all.

I rebuilt DRAKON’s re-entry screening to use multiple signals in combination: BSTAR drag coefficient trends, mean motion derivatives, altitude history, maneuver detection, and F10.7 solar flux for atmospheric density changes. No single metric is reliable on its own. BSTAR is noisy, altitude alone misses early decay, and accounting for solar flux significantly improves estimates below roughly 500 km.

Each object gets a confidence score reflecting how consistently the signals agree. A high-confidence object isn’t just losing altitude fast; its BSTAR trend, mean motion derivative, and multi-epoch regression all point the same direction. When they diverge, the score reflects that uncertainty rather than forcing a verdict. From there, objects are assigned risk tiers: terminal, high, elevated, moderate, and stable.

For terminal and high-tier objects, DRAKON estimates days remaining to re-entry. Validated against US Space Force TIP data and aerospace.org records, the estimates typically land within a few days of published predictions. The accuracy comes from regression detecting acceleration in the decay curve, not extrapolating from a single snapshot.

The model uses exponentially weighted regression, giving recent epochs more weight while older observations anchor the fit.

Low-altitude objects needed separate handling. Below around 400 km, drag increases fast enough that a week-old trend can be meaningfully wrong about what happens next. For those, altitude becomes the dominant predictor, while trend data is incorporated only when it produces the more conservative estimate.

DRAKON now ranks actively decaying objects in roughly the order they’ll likely re-enter, with confidence scores, risk tiers, and day-level timing estimates. That includes operational payloads, not just debris.

That’s because altitude, by itself, tells you very little about how fast an orbit is actually decaying.

What really matters is the combination of drag, orbital geometry, and an atmosphere that never stays the same. Perigee altitude determines where most of the drag happens. The ballistic coefficient, driven largely by mass-to-area ratio, determines how much that drag slows the satellite down.

On top of that, the thermosphere expands and contracts with solar activity. So two objects sitting at almost the same mean altitude can lose energy at completely different rates, and you would never know just by looking at the altitude.

The reason decay speeds up over time comes down to how quickly the atmosphere gets denser as you go lower. A drop of just a few tens of kilometers can increase drag by several times. As drag lowers the orbit, the satellite spends more time in denser air, which creates a feedback loop. More drag leads to a lower orbit, which leads to even more drag. That’s why orbital decay is rarely linear, even though it’s tempting to draw a straight line from the first few days of data.

Eccentricity makes the picture even more interesting. Imagine a satellite with a 220 km perigee and a 520 km apogee. It loses most of its orbital energy during a short pass near perigee each orbit. Another satellite with the same average altitude but a more circular orbit could last much longer simply because it spends far less time skimming through the thickest part of the atmosphere.

One thing this taught me is that re-entry prediction is never something you calculate once and forget about. It’s an estimate that keeps changing as the atmosphere changes and as new tracking data comes in. The hardest part is building a system that keeps updating its predictions instead of trusting a single snapshot.
